# Step 1 — Retention Pass Proposal

**Draft source:** `trojan_war_script_20min_with_dialogue.txt`  
**Status:** Proposed only — do not treat any wording below as approved VO text.

The draft has a strong chronological spine, concise dialogue, and a compelling final evidence section. The edits below are deliberately limited to the master brief's permitted Step 1 categories: a sharper opening, curiosity gaps, part recap-hooks/cliffhangers, and occasional pattern interrupts. The underlying history, tone, dialogue attribution, and ending are unchanged.

## Proposed spoken-script changes

### 1. Sharpen the cold open

**Before**
> About three thousand two hundred years ago, a walled city on a windy hill in what is now Turkey burned to the ground. What came after is a mix of poetry, memory, and shovel work. The Greeks said it fell over one woman. Their poets blamed a grudge between goddesses. Archaeologists point to something far less romantic. So which one is it? To find out, we start where the Greeks started: with a wedding, and a guest list that went very wrong.

**After**
> One wooden horse. One sleeping city. One night that ended in fire. But the story of Troy did not begin at the gates. It began three thousand two hundred years ago with a wedding invitation, one furious goddess, and a choice that pulled an entire world into war. The Greeks blamed one woman. Their poets blamed a grudge between goddesses. Archaeologists point to something far less romantic. So which version is closest to the truth? Start with the guest list.

**Why:** Puts the iconic payoff in the first seconds, creates a clear question, then snaps back to the inciting incident.

---

### 2. Make Paris's prize a stronger open loop

**Before**
> Aphrodite got the apple. The most beautiful woman alive was named Helen. And Helen was already married.

**After**
> Aphrodite got the apple. The most beautiful woman alive was named Helen. She was already married — and that marriage came with a promise that could summon the kings of Greece.

**Why:** Plants the oath before Chapter 2 and gives the viewer a reason to keep watching.

---

### 3. Strengthen the first-part exit without changing its event

**Before**
> The wind rose. The fleet sailed. And Clytemnestra, back in Mycenae, began to wait.

**After**
> The wind rose. The fleet sailed. And Clytemnestra, back in Mycenae, began to wait — while across the sea, a war that was supposed to be quick settled in for ten long years.

**Why:** Converts a quiet departure into a time-jump cliffhanger for the next part/chapter.

---

### 4. Add a five-second Part 2 recap-hook at Chapter 4

**Before**
> By Homer's count, close to twelve hundred ships crossed the Aegean. According to tradition, the first man to step ashore was destined to die.

**After**
> A wedding, an oath, and a sacrifice had launched the largest fleet Greece could imagine. But reaching Troy was the easy part. By Homer's count, close to twelve hundred ships crossed the Aegean. According to tradition, the first man to step ashore was destined to die.

**Why:** Rapidly resets the stakes after a part break and turns the first landing into an immediate danger beat.

---

### 5. Turn Achilles's withdrawal into a clearer promise of consequences

**Before**
> Zeus agreed. And the tide of the war turned.

**After**
> Zeus agreed. The Greeks had spent nine years trying to break Troy. Now their best warrior would sit out — and the tide of the war would turn against them.

**Why:** Clarifies the consequence at the hinge of the Iliad and opens a question that Chapter 6 immediately answers.

---

### 6. Add a Part 3 recap-hook at Chapter 8

**Before**
> On the twelfth night, an old man crossed the plain alone.

**After**
> Hector was dead, but Troy's walls still stood. Before the war could end, its greatest Greek warrior would have to fall too. On the twelfth night, an old man crossed the plain alone.

**Why:** Re-enters on the emotional aftermath, previews Achilles's fate, and makes Priam's approach feel even more tense.

---

### 7. Sharpen the transition from the Iliad to the later Trojan-cycle stories

**Before**
> The Iliad ends right there, with the burial of Hector, tamer of horses. Not with a victory. But the war was not over.

**After**
> The Iliad ends there, with the burial of Hector, tamer of horses — not with a victory. But the war was not over. The stories that follow would kill Achilles, exhaust every prophecy, and leave Troy facing one final impossible choice.

**Why:** Makes the boundary between Homer and later sources explicit while setting up the next act.

---

### 8. Make the horse plan a cleaner end-of-part cliffhanger

**Before**
> The Trojans pulled the horse inside. They celebrated all night, and then they slept.

**After**
> The Trojans pulled the horse inside. They celebrated all night, and then they slept — unaware that the war had finally crossed their own threshold.

**Why:** Ends the horse sequence on a direct, ominous cliffhanger without revealing the next action too early.

---

### 9. Add a five-second Part 4 recap-hook at Chapter 11

**Before**
> SINON: (quietly, at the horse) "Now."

**After**
> Ten years of siege had failed. One night of trust would do what an army could not.  
> SINON: (quietly, at the horse) "Now."

**Why:** A compact recap-hook that launches directly into the fall of Troy.

---

### 10. Use a deliberate myth-versus-evidence pattern interrupt at Chapter 13

**Before**
> Is any of it real? In northwest Turkey there is a mound called Hisarlik.

**After**
> Pause the gods, the prophecies, and the wooden horse for a moment. Is any of it real? In northwest Turkey there is a mound called Hisarlik.

**Why:** Signals the shift from epic story to archaeological evidence and refreshes attention before the final answer.
