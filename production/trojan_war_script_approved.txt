THE TROJAN WAR: THE FULL STORY (DIALOGUE VERSION, APPROX. 20-22 MINUTES)

FORMAT KEY
- Plain paragraphs = narrator.
- SPEAKER: "line" = a character speaking. Text in (parentheses) is a delivery note and is not spoken.
- MASCOT: "line" = a short interjection from the skeleton mascot.
- Lines in [BRACKETS] are chapter titles for structure only and are not spoken.

[COLD OPEN]

One wooden horse. One sleeping city. One night that ended in fire. But the story of Troy did not begin at the gates. It began three thousand two hundred years ago with a wedding invitation, one furious goddess, and a choice that pulled an entire world into war. The Greeks blamed one woman. Their poets blamed a grudge between goddesses. Archaeologists point to something far less romantic. So which version is closest to the truth? Start with the guest list.

[CHAPTER 1: THE UNINVITED GUEST]

The groom was a mortal king named Peleus. The bride was Thetis, a sea goddess so lovely that Zeus himself had wanted her, until he heard a prophecy. Her son, it said, would grow up greater than his father. Suddenly no god wanted anything to do with her. Marry her to a mortal, they decided, and the problem goes away.

So Olympus came to the wedding. Music, wine, mountains of food. And one missing guest. Eris, the goddess of strife, had not been invited.

MASCOT: "Spoiler. Terrible idea."

She showed up anyway.

ERIS: (sweetly) "Don't mind me. I only came to drop off a gift."

She rolled a golden apple across the floor. Scratched into it were the words: for the most beautiful.

Three goddesses reached for it at once.

HERA: "Obviously it's mine. I'm the queen of heaven."

ATHENA: "Beauty fades. Wisdom doesn't. Hand it over."

APHRODITE: "Please. I'm the goddess of beauty. It practically has my name on it."

They turned to Zeus, and Zeus, who had not stayed king by being foolish, wanted no part of it.

ZEUS: "Pick one of you, and I make two enemies. Not a chance. Find a mortal."

Far away on Mount Ida, a young shepherd named Paris was minding his flock. He was secretly a prince of Troy. At his birth, his mother had dreamed her baby would set the city on fire, so the king ordered him left on a mountainside. Troy had tried to avoid its own destruction, and ended up raising its destroyer in a hut.

Each goddess came to him alone, with an offer.

HERA: "Choose me, and you rule every kingdom between here and the sunrise."

ATHENA: "Choose me, and no one will ever beat you in battle."

APHRODITE: "Choose me, and the most beautiful woman alive is yours."

PARIS: (after a very short pause) "Sorry, Hera. Sorry, Athena."

Aphrodite got the apple. The most beautiful woman alive was named Helen. She was already married — and that marriage came with a promise that could summon the kings of Greece.

[CHAPTER 2: THE OATH]

Helen was the daughter of Tyndareus, king of Sparta, and when she came of age, nearly every important man in Greece arrived to ask for her hand. Tyndareus panicked.

TYNDAREUS: "Whoever I choose, the rest will tear Greece apart."

The man who solved it was Odysseus, who had no real chance at Helen himself.

ODYSSEUS: "Then make them swear first. Whoever wins, every other suitor defends his marriage. And in return, help me win Penelope."

Tyndareus sacrificed a horse, and the suitors swore standing over its remains.

MASCOT: "Keep that horse in mind."

Helen chose Menelaus, a king with a famous brother. That brother was Agamemnon of Mycenae, the most powerful ruler in Greece.

Years later, Paris arrived in Sparta as a guest. Menelaus was called away to Crete, and when he came home, his wife and a good part of his treasure were gone. Did Helen leave willingly, or was she taken? The Greeks never agreed. In Homer, Helen blames herself bitterly for the rest of her life.

MENELAUS: (shaking) "He sat at my table. He ate my food. And he took my wife."

AGAMEMNON: "Then we call in the oath. Every king who ever courted Helen sails with us."

Except that some of them did not want to go.

[CHAPTER 3: THE FLEET AT AULIS]

Odysseus, king of Ithaca, had a new baby and a happy home. When the recruiters arrived, he pretended to be out of his mind. He yoked a donkey and an ox to a plow and began sowing the beach with salt.

A man named Palamedes saw through it. He laid Odysseus's infant son in front of the plow.

PALAMEDES: "Keep plowing, my king. Straight ahead."

Odysseus swerved.

ODYSSEUS: (through his teeth) "All right. You made your point. I'll go."

He never forgave Palamedes, which, as we will see, ended badly for Palamedes.

The Greeks also needed Achilles, the best warrior of his generation. His mother Thetis knew he was fated to die at Troy, so she hid him among the daughters of a king on the island of Skyros, where he fell in love and fathered a son named Neoptolemus.

THETIS: "Stay hidden, my son. If you go to Troy, you will not come home."

ACHILLES: "A long life that nobody remembers, or a short one that nobody forgets. It's not much of a choice, Mother."

The fleet gathered at a harbor called Aulis, and then it sat there. No wind. Day after day, the sails hung limp.

CALCHAS: "Artemis is angry. The wind returns when your daughter Iphigenia is sacrificed."

AGAMEMNON: "Not her. Anyone but her."

CALCHAS: "Then the ships stay where they are."

Agamemnon sent word that Iphigenia should come to the camp, to marry Achilles. She arrived in her wedding dress.

IPHIGENIA: "Father? Where is the groom?"

What happened next depends on who is telling it. In some versions she was killed. In others, Artemis swapped her for a deer at the last second and carried her away.

The wind rose. The fleet sailed. And Clytemnestra, back in Mycenae, began to wait — while across the sea, a war that was supposed to be quick settled in for ten long years.

[CHAPTER 4: NINE YEARS OF NOTHING]

A wedding, an oath, and a sacrifice had launched the largest fleet Greece could imagine. But reaching Troy was the easy part. By Homer's count, close to twelve hundred ships crossed the Aegean. According to tradition, the first man to step ashore was destined to die.

PROTESILAUS: "Someone has to be first."

He was dead within the hour.

Early on, another leader, Philoctetes, was bitten by a snake. The wound smelled so foul that the army abandoned him on the island of Lemnos, along with his bow.

PHILOCTETES: "You can't leave me here!"

ODYSSEUS: "Your wound is too foul for the camp. We'll send for you."

That bow had belonged to Heracles. They would regret leaving it.

Then came the long middle. Troy's walls were said to have been raised by Poseidon and Apollo, who had been cheated of their payment by an earlier king, so at least two gods had a grudge against the city. The Greeks raided the surrounding land for years.

ACHILLES: "A dozen cities by sea. Eleven more by land. And Troy's walls still stand."

Nine years of this. And then, in the tenth, it all nearly fell apart. Not because of Troy, but because of an argument in the Greek camp.

[CHAPTER 5: THE ARGUMENT]

The Iliad does not open with a battle. It opens with a plague. Agamemnon had taken a captive girl, Chryseis, whose father served as a priest of Apollo.

CHRYSES: "Give me back my daughter."

AGAMEMNON: "Leave my camp, old man, before I lose my patience."

The priest prayed to his god, and Apollo's arrows fell on the Greek camp for nine days. Agamemnon finally gave in.

AGAMEMNON: "Fine. She goes home. But I'll take Briseis in her place."

ACHILLES: "After everything I've done for you? You'd take from me?"

AGAMEMNON: "I'm the king. I take what I choose."

Achilles reached for his sword. Athena herself caught him by the hair, and he held back. But he left the field, and he sat in his tent, and he asked his mother for something terrible.

ACHILLES: "Mother, ask Zeus to let them lose. Let them find out what they owe me."

Zeus agreed. The Greeks had spent nine years trying to break Troy. Now their best warrior would sit out — and the tide of the war would turn against them.

[CHAPTER 6: A DUEL AND A GOODBYE]

First, a pause. With the armies lined up, Paris and Menelaus agreed to settle everything in single combat. Winner takes Helen. Menelaus was winning, with one hand on Paris's helmet, when Aphrodite snapped the strap and swept Paris away in a mist. She set him down in his bedroom.

Helen watched all this from the walls.

HELEN: (cold) "So you're alive. I almost hoped the battle would end this."

The truce collapsed anyway, and the fighting began again.

Then comes the quietest scene in the whole poem. Hector, prince of Troy and its best fighter, walks back into the city to say goodbye to his wife, Andromache, and their baby son, Astyanax.

ANDROMACHE: "You are my husband, my father, my brothers. Please don't go back out there."

HECTOR: "If I hide behind these walls, I could never face my own people."

He reaches for his boy, and the child screams. His father's helmet has a plume that looks like a monster. Hector laughs, lifts the helmet off, and sets it on the ground.

HECTOR: "May my son grow up better than his father."

Everyone listening knew how this was going to end.

[CHAPTER 7: ARMOR]

Without Achilles, the Greeks were crushed. Agamemnon sent three envoys to beg him, with gifts, and Achilles refused. He said he was thinking of sailing home.

Hector reached the beach. The first Greek ship caught fire. And Achilles's closest friend, Patroclus, could not watch any longer. He asked to wear Achilles's armor and lead his men.

ACHILLES: "Drive them from the ships. Then come back. Do not chase them to the city."

PATROCLUS: "I understand."

He did not. Patroclus drove the Trojans to their own walls, and there Apollo knocked the helmet from his head. A Trojan spear wounded him, and Hector finished the job.

When Achilles heard, he lay on the ground and poured dust over his head. His mother brought a new set of armor, made by the god Hephaestus, with a shield that showed the whole world on it. Achilles made peace with Agamemnon, and went to find Hector.

He killed a river's worth of Trojans. The river god Scamander, choked with bodies, rose against him, and only fire from the gods turned it back. The Trojans fled into the city. All except Hector, who stood alone outside the gate.

Then his nerve broke. He ran. Achilles chased him around the walls again and again, until Athena, disguised as Hector's own brother, convinced him to turn and fight.

HECTOR: "If I lose, swear you'll give my body back to my family."

ACHILLES: "Lions don't make bargains with men."

The spear went through his neck. Achilles tied the body behind his chariot and dragged it around Patroclus's tomb every morning, for twelve days.

[CHAPTER 8: THE KING IN THE TENT]

Hector was dead, but Troy's walls still stood. Before the war could end, its greatest Greek warrior would have to fall too. On the twelfth night, an old man crossed the plain alone. Priam, king of Troy, had come to the Greek camp, led by the god Hermes, carrying a ransom. He walked past the guards, into the tent of the man who had killed his son, and knelt. He took Achilles's hands and kissed them.

PRIAM: "Think of your own father, Achilles. He is old, like me."

Achilles did. The two of them wept in the same room, one for Hector and one for Patroclus.

ACHILLES: "Sit, old king. Eat. Even Niobe ate, after she lost all her children."

He gave back the body and promised a truce for the funeral. The Iliad ends there, with the burial of Hector, tamer of horses — not with a victory. But the war was not over. The stories that follow would kill Achilles, exhaust every prophecy, and leave Troy facing one final impossible choice.

[CHAPTER 9: THE DEATH OF ACHILLES]

The rest comes from lost poems and later writers. Two more allies arrived to help Troy. An Amazon queen named Penthesilea, whom Achilles killed, and then, the story says, regretted. Then Memnon, a king from the east and the son of the goddess of dawn. Achilles killed him too.

But Paris, the shepherd who started it all, drew his bow at the city gates. The arrow struck Achilles, guided by Apollo, and the greatest warrior of the Greeks was dead. The famous heel does not appear in any of the early poems. A Roman poet added it more than a thousand years later.

Achilles's divine armor now needed an owner. Ajax, the huge, stubborn warrior who had carried the body from the field, was sure it was his.

AJAX: "I stood over his body while the arrows fell. Those weapons are mine."

ODYSSEUS: "And I held the line behind you. Let the judges decide."

The Greeks gave it to Odysseus. Ajax lost his reason and slaughtered a flock of livestock, thinking they were his own commanders. When he came to his senses, he could not bear what he had done, and he took his own life.

Then a captured Trojan seer told the Greeks what they still needed.

HELENUS: "You need three things. Achilles's son. The statue of Athena inside the city. And the bow of Heracles. Without them, Troy stands forever."

They sent for all three. Philoctetes was healed and went back to the war. His first target was Paris. The arrow struck, and Paris, dying, begged for help from Oenone, a nymph he had abandoned years before.

OENONE: "You left me for her. And now you want my help?"

She refused. She changed her mind too late, and she took her own life beside his funeral fire.

Odysseus and Diomedes crept into Troy at night and stole the statue. Every prophecy was now fulfilled. And Troy's walls still stood.

[CHAPTER 10: THE HORSE]

ODYSSEUS: "We can't break their walls. So we make them open the gates themselves."

The Greeks would build a giant wooden horse and leave it outside the gate, as an offering to Athena for a safe voyage home. Inside, they would hide their best men. The rest would burn the camp and sail away, hiding behind a nearby island.

EPEIUS: "Give me pine from Mount Ida and three days."

A craftsman named Epeius built it, with Athena's help. The story does not agree on how many men fit inside. Some say twenty. Some say fifty. Nobody says it was comfortable.

In the morning, the Trojans walked out to find the Greek camp gone and an enormous horse standing in the sand. They argued.

TROJAN: "Burn it."

ANOTHER TROJAN: "Break it open."

THIRD TROJAN: "Drag it inside. It's a gift to Athena."

A priest named Laocoön had no doubt.

LAOCOON: "I don't trust Greeks, even when they bring gifts."

He threw a spear into its side, and the horse rang hollow. But that night, two sea serpents rose from the water and killed him and his sons. The Trojans took it as a sign.

A Greek prisoner named Sinon helped them decide.

SINON: "They left me to die. I'm telling you the truth. Keep the horse, and Athena protects you."

Cassandra, a daughter of Priam, told them the truth. Apollo had given her the gift of prophecy and the curse of never being believed.

CASSANDRA: "There are men inside. Burn it, before it burns you."

CROWD: "Quiet, Cassandra. Not tonight."

And the Odyssey adds a detail that gives me chills. Helen, in the dark, walked around the horse and called out to the men inside, in the voices of their own wives.

HELEN: (softly, in a stranger's voice) "Is that you, my love? Come out. It's me."

The men inside went still. One of them started to answer. Odysseus clamped a hand over his mouth.

ODYSSEUS: (barely a whisper) "Not a sound."

He held it there until she went away. The Trojans pulled the horse inside. They celebrated all night, and then they slept — unaware that the war had finally crossed their own threshold.

[CHAPTER 11: THE NIGHT TROY BURNED]

Ten years of siege had failed. One night of trust would do what an army could not.

SINON: (quietly, at the horse) "Now."

The warriors slid down, killed the guards, and opened the gates. The fleet, which had only been waiting offshore, came back in the dark.

Priam was found at the altar of his own palace, face to face with Neoptolemus, the son of Achilles.

PRIAM: "Your father at least respected an old man."

NEOPTOLEMUS: "Then go and tell him how his son behaves."

Astyanax, the baby who had screamed at his father's helmet, was thrown from the walls so that he would never grow up to take revenge. The Trojan princess Polyxena was sacrificed at Achilles's tomb. The women of Troy, including Hector's widow, Andromache, were carried off as slaves.

And Cassandra was dragged from the statue of Athena by Ajax the Lesser, a crime so serious that Athena, who had backed the Greeks for ten years, turned on them completely.

Troy was gone. But in the Greek story, winning was the easy part.

[CHAPTER 12: THE ROAD HOME]

The goddess sent storms that scattered the fleet. Ajax the Lesser clung to a rock.

AJAX THE LESSER: "I survived! The gods couldn't stop me!"

POSEIDON: "Say that again."

Poseidon split the rock with his trident.

Agamemnon sailed home to Mycenae in triumph. His wife Clytemnestra was waiting, with her new lover, Aegisthus, and ten years of grief for Iphigenia.

CLYTEMNESTRA: "Ten years, Agamemnon. Did you think I wasn't counting?"

AGAMEMNON: "I won the war. I'm home."

CLYTEMNESTRA: "Yes. And now you answer for our daughter."

Some versions say he was murdered at a banquet. Others, in his bath. The man who commanded the largest army of the age did not survive his first night home. His son Orestes, years later, avenged him.

Menelaus and Helen wandered for eight years, through Egypt and beyond, before they made it back to Sparta. And Odysseus took another ten years, through monsters and witches and a few angry gods. But that is another story.

On the Trojan side, one man escaped. Aeneas carried his old father out of the burning city on his back. Centuries later, the Roman poet Virgil said that Aeneas reached Italy and became the ancestor of Rome. One of the greatest empires in history traced its roots to a refugee from a burned city.

[CHAPTER 13: SO WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED?]

Pause the gods, the prophecies, and the wooden horse for a moment. Is any of it real? In northwest Turkey there is a mound called Hisarlik. A British diplomat and amateur archaeologist, Frank Calvert, suspected it was Troy. Then a wealthy German businessman, Heinrich Schliemann, dug through it in the 1870s with a trench so aggressive that he cut straight through the layers he was looking for.

SCHLIEMANN: (triumphant) "I have found the treasure of Priam!"

He had not. It was about a thousand years too early.

But the site is real. Nine main cities were built on top of each other. One of them, called Troy VIIa, was burned around 1180 BC, with human remains and piles of sling stones scattered in the ruins. Something violent did happen there.

And the Hittite archives, from the same period, add something remarkable. They mention a western kingdom called Wilusa, which sounds a lot like Ilios, the Greek name for Troy. A treaty names its king, Alaksandu, which sounds a lot like Alexandros, another name for Paris. They also mention a rival power across the sea, called Ahhiyawa, which may be the Achaeans.

MASCOT: "That's a lot of coincidences."

The war did not necessarily happen the way Homer told it. It may have been many raids, over many years, folded into one story. But something happened on that hill, bad enough that nobody ever stopped telling it.

In the Greek version, it all began with a guest list. A party, and one goddess nobody thought to invite.
